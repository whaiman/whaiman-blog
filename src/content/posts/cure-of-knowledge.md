## Definition

**The curse of knowledge** is a cognitive bias: once you know something, you struggle to picture what it's like not to know it.

New knowledge folds into your model of the world, and the brain has trouble rebuilding the old, uninformed view. Things that stay genuinely obscure to other people start to feel obvious to you.

## The core of it

Learning something new means passing through a string of intermediate stages: half-understandings, wrong turns, moments of confusion. Once the material is mastered, those stages fade from memory.

An expert remembers the outcome of years of study, not which questions felt hard at the start. That gap - between what the explainer knows and what the listener knows - is where the trouble starts. The expert assumes the other person already holds far more background than they do, so the explanation comes out incomplete. Details, definitions, and logical steps go missing, because they seemed too basic to mention.

## The classic experiment

One of the best-known demonstrations of this bias is the tapping experiment.

Participants picked a well-known song and tapped its rhythm on a table with their fingers. Before starting, they guessed how often a listener would recognize the tune. Most expected listeners to get it right about half the time.

The real numbers came in far lower: listeners recognized the song in only 2-3% of attempts.

The reason is simple. The person tapping hears the full melody in their head while they tap - every beat carries meaning, tied to a song they already know. The listener hears none of that: just a string of taps, no melody attached, no context. They can't hear the music playing in the tapper's head.

The experiment makes the point on its own: knowledge and expectation can distort what we think other people are able to understand.

## Showing up in teaching

The curse of knowledge shows up all over education.

In one line of research, experts were asked to estimate how long beginners would need to learn a given skill. The experts came in low, again and again. The material looked simple to them, since they'd known it for years.

The pattern often runs backward from what you'd expect: the more expertise someone has, the more they underestimate how hard the material is for a beginner.

## Why it happens

The curse of knowledge comes down to how memory and thinking work. Once information becomes familiar:

- the sense of how hard it was to learn fades
- the feeling of novelty disappears
- the intermediate steps of understanding get forgotten
- a false sense of obviousness sets in

Psychologists sometimes call this the "illusion of transparency": if something is clear to you, it feels like it should be clear to everyone else too.
